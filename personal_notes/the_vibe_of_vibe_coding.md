# The Vibe of Vide Coding

Let's start with a quick history lesson: the term \"Vibe Coding\" was first thrown into the world by Andrej Karpathy. He basically sparked this whole conversation—probably while vibing through a midnight coding session himself.

[IMAGE_PLACEHOLDER:vibe_coding.png]

When I first kicked off [HackerComments.ai](http://HackerComments.ai), Python was my obvious go-to—because, duh, that's what I know. In no time, I had a tiny prototype up, pulling data, sorting it out, and using AI to sift through and extract the juicy bits I needed. Back then, Cursor wasn't really a thing yet, and I was way too stingy for GitHub Copilot. So what did I do? Simple. I copied chunks of my code straight into ChatGPT, bug by bug, fixing tiny mistakes one at a time. It felt great—like playing whack-a-mole but less violent and more satisfying because I didn't have to wrestle with whatever crazy algorithm LeetCode would've made me memorize for \"optimal performance.\"

With the backend logic figured out, I moved to building bricks—setting up Django, managing migrations, creating endpoints. \"Piece of cake,\" I thought, right until deployment. Suddenly, things had to actually *click* together, and that's when reality got real. My ChatGPT sessions got dangerously long—so much that sometimes the MacOS client jammed, and I'd panic, terrified of losing my precious conversation history. Imagine being emotionally attached to a single ChatGPT session—that was me.

Then, inevitably, frontend came knocking, and let me tell you, frontend and I aren't friends. To spare myself some pain, I blatantly copied Hacker News's UI—with just a tiny twist, like that slide-to-open-content thingy. Honestly, even that scared me, because I was clueless about frontend. Docs were readable, sure, but turning that into a working product felt like climbing Mount Everest wearing flip-flops. At this stage, my beloved ChatGPT copy-paste routine hit its limits.

That's exactly when Cursor walked in like a cool kid saving me from frontend despair. Suddenly, efficiency skyrocketed just by ditching the \"copy\" part. Did I enjoy it? Yes and no. Honestly, I had no idea if my prompts sucked or my designs were garbage—I kept iterating, especially when tweaking features like screen split ratios. Those nights of \"vibe coding\" blurred together into an endless loop of tweaking, testing, and hoping. A small side-note: LLM rarely screws up syntax (though it does happen), but catching logical errors or misuse can be insanely tough if you don't fully grasp the code. Ask me how I know.

Fast forward to today, and I'm somehow handling two projects simultaneously. One gets my full attention, with detailed feature tweaking, while the other relies heavily on developer tools to auto-build the skeleton. Basically, I'm vibe coding in parallel universes.

**So, circling back to Andrej's definition of vibe coding:**

- I'm a lone wolf dev cultivating a small digital garden (not calling myself a founder yet—I'm not even close to properly defining a problem that makes money).
- No need to hire frontend or backend ninjas because I'm just out here solo-building an MVP.
- Basically, just vibing my way through code and seeing where it takes me.

Am I getting the vibe right?

But here's the plot twist: the closer you get to turning your vibe-coded MVP into reality, the faster the initial thrill fades. Sure, \"Vibe Coding\" feels amazing when you're staring at a blank canvas, but trust me, reality will slap you hard at 2 AM when you're wrestling with frontend bugs or backend nightmares (this literaly happen on March 29 Sat. 2025). It's exhilarating, yes, but also humbling—a gentle reminder that even in 2025, reliable, scalable software still needs humans who know what they're doing.

Stay tuned, because that's where the real vibe coding story starts.
